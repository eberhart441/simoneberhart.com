I've spent a bit over two years trying to drive a six-axis robot arm with one motor. The early versions moved, but making the clutches small, strong, and quick enough has been a longer fight than I expected. I'm still building it. I don't have torque or cycle-time figures worth publishing yet. I'm building it into a product and plan to raise money for it.

1Mechanism

A conventional robot arm puts a motor at each joint. I put one motor on a shared drive shaft, then use clutches to connect that shaft to whichever joint needs to move. Engage a clutch, drive the motor, disengage it, and select another axis.

The joints can be addressed separately, but they can't all demand independent torque at the same time. Motion has to be scheduled around the one motor. That's a substantial limitation, although for some kinds of manipulation it may be worth trading simultaneous motion for fewer actuators.

I initially thought getting rid of five motors would make the arm much simpler. In practice, I just moved most of the difficulty into the clutches. They have to engage under the right conditions, release without sticking, and do it often enough that the arm doesn't spend all its time waiting to move. Backlash and torque capacity have also been persistent problems. The mechanism is much less forgiving in metal than it is in CAD.

2Clutches

The first design used hobby servos to slide the clutches into place. It was a good way to test the idea, but the servos took up an absurd amount of space. Next I designed my own solenoids, including the coil and magnet arrangement. I learned a lot about electromagnets and spent far too long making them. The newer versions use off-the-shelf solenoids to engage mechanical clutches, so I can work on the transmission instead of constantly redesigning the actuator that shifts it. Some of this work led to provisional patent filings.

3Electronics

I made the solenoid driver PCB and wrote its firmware. It's responsible for switching the clutches, which has to be coordinated with motor motion; driving into a clutch that's only half engaged is a good way to damage something. Two board views are below. You can rotate them and switch layers.

The control software is written. The harder part now is getting it to behave consistently with the current mechanical design.

4Next

Originally I was designing the whole arm around this transmission. That meant building a robot just to test one mechanism, which got old. I'm now working on an add-on for Universal Robots arms instead. The robot handles arm positioning; my module handles the hand. The plan is to keep the motors, encoders, and electronics in a common housing, then swap passive, cable-driven hands onto it. A clutch interface connects the new hand to the drive shafts.

I want to get a working prototype mounted on a Universal Robots arm before raising money. First I need to see how reliably the clutches engage, how much torque gets through, and whether swapping hands is actually as useful as I think it will be.
