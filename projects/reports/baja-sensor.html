I'm building a fuel gauge for Olin's Baja car that reads the tank's vibration, with nothing inside the tank. The tank sits on rubber mounts and vibrates when the car gets knocked around. As fuel is used, its effective mass changes, so its resonant frequency should shift. The idea is to measure that shift and infer the fuel level. I've designed the boards and firmware; they're being fabricated now. We're currently working on testing it with the car.

1Method

One accelerometer goes near the tank. The other goes on the chassis, about six inches away. Both measure the car shaking, but only the first should pick up much of the tank's motion relative to its mounts. I subtract the chassis measurement from the tank measurement and run an FFT on the residual to look for a resonance peak. With calibration at known fuel levels, the peak frequency might give me a usable fuel estimate.

There are obvious ways this could go wrong. The sensors need to be sampled in sync, the chassis vibration won't cancel perfectly, and sloshing fuel could move the peak around. I'll have a much better idea once I've collected data on the car.

The sensor boards share an internal CAN bus. One acts as the master and sends the processed fuel estimate to the car's logger over a separate CAN interface. I wanted to use the same board design at both locations rather than make a special version for each sensor.

2PCB

The board is four layers and 59 × 48 mm. This viewer uses the actual fabrication files, so you can look at the copper, silkscreen, and the opposite side instead of a rendered mockup.

3Schematic

An STM32F446 reads an MPU-6050 accelerometer over I²C. I included two CAN transceivers: INTER for the sensor network and EXT for the vehicle bus. Both interfaces have filtering and transient protection. There's also an SWD header and test pads for bringing the board up. The board also regulates power from the car's 12 V supply. That and the transient protection took more thought than I'd anticipated for a pair of accelerometers.
